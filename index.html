The decisions that cost real money at launch are the ones made without evidence: the wrong channel, the wrong price, the wrong first market, the wrong first country. Most of them can be checked before you pay for them.

Positioning, and the story you tell.

What the product is, who it is for, and the sentence that makes someone want it. Most launches are decided here, and it is usually the part nobody owns. It rarely lands on the first attempt, which is fine as long as you can tell.

A launch that answers questions instead of raising them.

I don't arrive with the answers about your business, and nobody has them before the market speaks. Post-Launch Audit

Technically live, but people don't install, don't pay, and don't come back.

+

The product is live and the numbers say nothing good. Installs don't come, users don't pay, or they pay once and never return, and the unit economics are nowhere near where they need to be. Either you don't know what to do next, or you know twenty things to do and have the resources for three. Normally one of four things is wrong: the positioning, the channel, the product, or the measurement telling you about the other three.

How it runs

  • I go through your analytics, funnel, ad accounts, store listings and creative myself

How I work

Deep in one thing, useful across many.

I go deep on growth economics: CAC, LTV, cohorts, payback. Around that core I work across product, positioning, lifecycle, content, PR, and operations. T-shaped on purpose.

Most growth problems aren't marketing problems.

They're positioning, product friction, or incentive problems. My job is to find the real constraint before spending budget on the wrong one.

Goals first, tactics second.

Before a feature or a channel test I want the success metric agreed: what we are looking for, what we will test, what counts as a good result, and what is not good enough. It is asking why several times before anyone builds anything. Skip it and you settle for results that look fine and bring no more money, no more returning users, and no more use of the product.

At zero there is no data and no playbook.

Only hypotheses to test and uncertainties to reveal. So you stay open to changing your mind, and you move quickly — not because speed is a virtue in itself, but because the faster you test, the sooner you actually know where to go. At the start, certainty is something you earn by moving, not something you have before you begin.

I do the work, and I hire when the work needs it.

I have started as an individual contributor more than once and I am comfortable as a player-coach. Early on I do a lot myself: it saves the time coordination costs, and often I do not yet know who to hire. That becomes clear when a channel starts running on its own — Meta is growing, needs attention every day, and my time for new experiments shrinks. Then I add someone, for work that will still exist when things change, not for an org chart. AI has moved that line further out: more gets done with fewer people and less time, and the judgement calls are still mine. This site is one small example — I built it myself, with AI.

I'll take the risk when the data isn't coming.

I measure everything that can be measured, and I estimate the rest: how much goes unattributed, and where the untraceable demand went. But there is always uncertainty, and sometimes the data is never going to arrive — too few users for a test that would mean anything. Then I decide. I push the uncertainty down as far as every available tool will take it, I say plainly what I am not sure about, and I move. Growth is not a guarantee business, and waiting for certainty costs more than being wrong early.
